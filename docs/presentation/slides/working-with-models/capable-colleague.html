Working With Models

Treat Models Like a Colleague

Not a tool. Not an oracle. A capable colleague that works for you.

› Fast, and knows the language better than you do.
› Has no idea about your last meeting's decisions.
› Has the utmost confidence in their abilities.
› Will not tell you when they're unsure.

Models sound just as sure when they're wrong.

This is the mental model I'd most like people to leave with, and it took me a while to arrive at it. "Tool" is wrong because a tool doesn't make judgement calls — a hammer doesn't decide where the nail goes. "Oracle" is wrong in the other direction, and it's the more expensive mistake, because it stops you reviewing. Colleague is the closest fit. And once you hold that frame, the right behaviours follow without anyone having to prescribe them. You read what a colleague hands you. You ask why they chose that approach. You give them the context they'd need to do the job — which is exactly what a persona and a well-documented project are. The three properties matter in that order. The speed and the language fluency are genuine and they're why we're all here. The missing context is manageable, and it's what the rest of this talk has been about closing. The third one has no equivalent in a human colleague, and that's why it catches people. A colleague who is out of their depth signals it — they hesitate, they hedge, they ask a question. The model produces a confident answer at the edge of its knowledge in exactly the same tone as a confident answer at the centre of it. There is no tell. You cannot calibrate your trust by reading the output's confidence, because the confidence is constant. Which means the review is not optional, and it doesn't get to be selective either.
